A repeatable system for AI interior design

The problem

Ask an image model for a room three times and you get three rooms

The first attempt asked for three views of the loft on one canvas. The model redesigned the space in each panel.

Geometry drifts

  • A second window appeared
  • A door showed in a view where it should be behind the camera
  • Brick moved to the west wall in one panel

Light drifts

  • Golden-hour orange cast instead of 10:30 AM north light
  • Ceiling and beams cropped out of two views

Details drift

  • Jute-textured rug, stray pillows, nightstands, a pouf
  • The olive tree switched sides between views

Better adjectives did not fix this. A process did: fix the room first, specify every piece, and score each render against the brief.

Part 1

The system

Four steps and a reusable template. Each one exists because of a failure in the first render.

Step 1  ·  Lock the room

Fixed facts come from the floor plan, not from the model

Give the system a floor plan, blueprint, survey, or CAD export (image or PDF) and it extracts the fixed facts instead of asking for them. With no plan, they are typed in or measured. Either way, every fact is labeled as read from the plan, calculated, or assumed.

  1. Set the scaleRead a scale bar, a stated scale, or one labeled dimension. Scale is never inferred from furniture.
  2. Extract the fixed factsRoom dimensions, wall thickness, door and window positions from a named corner, plumbing and electrical, columns, stairs, and finishes from the legend. Ceiling height is often missing from plans, so sections and notes are checked.
  3. Label every factRead from the plan, calculated, or assumed. Only critical gaps get a question: north orientation, ceiling height, sill height, which wall is brick.
  4. Cross-checkDimensions add up, openings fit their walls, and the plan agrees with the description. Conflicts are settled before any image is generated.
  5. Convert to coordinates and textOne origin and one set of axes for every piece and camera. The plan goes into the prompt as words, because image models do not follow drawn plans reliably.

Plans can carry addresses, names, and permit numbers. Redact them before a plan is shown publicly.

Step 2  ·  Write the brief

Six pillars, filled in before any image is generated

Shown here with the loft's values.

01

Fixed facts

Dimensions, ceiling, window, walls, floor, openings and services. Typed in, measured, or extracted from an uploaded floor plan. Treated as unchangeable across every prompt.

02

Style ratio

60% Mid-Century Modern, 25% Industrial, 15% Western Americana. The industrial share comes from the building. The Western share is carried by material only: saddle leather, cowhide, aged brass.

03

Palette tied to light

North light is cool and flat, so the palette runs warm: cognac, burnt sienna, saddle tan, oatmeal, walnut, aged brass, with 2700K lamps as the only warm pools.

04

Key pieces

Every piece is written as material plus placement, for example "84-inch cognac top-grain leather sofa centered on the brick wall facing the window."

05

Constraints and exclusions

Renter-friendly, freestanding, moderate budget. No chrome or stainless, no barn wood, no animal skulls, no striped patterns, no upper cabinets or open kitchen shelving.

06

Output specification

5-ft eye level, 24 mm wide angle, f/8, straight verticals, 3:2, one image per generation, plus a room-zone text brief and a shopping table.

Step 3  ·  Prompt from the brief

Six rules for prompts that hold the room steady

Each rule exists because a render broke it. Examples are from the loft.

  1. Lock the shell firstGenerate the empty loft, then reuse its architecture block word for word in every "after" prompt. Consistency comes from repetition, not from asking the model to remember.
  2. Write material and placement, not nouns"A leather sofa" produces any sofa. "84-inch cognac top-grain leather sofa with tapered walnut legs, centered on the brick wall" produces a checkable one.
  3. Tie the palette to the lightThe window faces north, so daylight is cool. The prompt states that and names the warm counter-palette and bulb temperature, which also stopped the model from defaulting to golden hour.
  4. Spell out orientationEach shot line names which wall is on the left and right and what is behind the camera. The first render put brick on the wrong wall and a door in view until these were added.
  5. Name what must not appearNegative constraints came from reading failures: exactly one window, no pouf, no striped pillows, no stainless. Each is a line that exists because a render broke the rule.
  6. One image per generationThe first attempt asked for three views in one collage and the model reinvented the room per panel. Generating A1 alone, then using it as a reference for the other views, keeps the room stable.

Step 4  ·  Audit the render

Score every render against the brief, then patch

Each pillar becomes yes-or-no checks. A check scores 1 if the render matches, 0.5 if it partly matches, and 0 if it does not. The review is manual and visual, not a pixel measurement. The style ratio is a judgment call and is not scored, and true dimensions cannot be read from a render, so placement and relative scale are checked instead.

  1. Break the brief into checksOne check per specified fact: exactly one window, brick on the south wall only, a walnut slab coffee table with a black steel base.
  2. Score the render and record each missThe result is a score per pillar and a list of what failed, so the next change is targeted.
  3. Fix the prompt or the briefRendering errors become new prompt lines. Design problems, such as two lamps stacked at one end or a console with no purpose, change the brief itself.

Template

The reusable brief

Fill this in once per project and reuse it verbatim for every image. Download template.md

Six-pillar interior design brief 
### [Project Name / Concept Title]

#### 1. Fixed Facts (Ground Truth)
* Dimensions & Layout: [e.g., studio loft, 16x20 ft]
* Ceiling & Architecture: [e.g., 12-ft timber-beam ceiling, large factory window facing north]
* Walls & Flooring: [e.g., exposed red brick, polished concrete floors]
* Openings & Services: [door and window positions from a named corner; plumbing and electrical locations]
* Source: [floor plan upload, measured, or assumed; mark each fact [plan], [calc], or [assumed]]

#### 2. Style Ratio
* Breakdown: [e.g., 70% Mid-Century Modern, 20% Western Americana, 10% Industrial]

#### 3. Palette Tied to Light
* Light Adaptation: [e.g., countering cool north light with warm earth tones]

#### 4. Key Pieces (Material + Placement)
* Seating: [e.g., cognac top-grain leather sofa against the brick wall]
* Textiles: [e.g., brown-and-white cowhide layered under the coffee table]
* Lighting & Decor: [e.g., matte black iron arc lamp over the seating zone]

#### 5. Constraints and Exclusions
* Constraints: [e.g., renter-friendly, moderate budget]
* Exclusions: [e.g., no faux-rustic clutter, no chrome finishes]

#### 6. Output Specification
* Image Prompt Specs: [e.g., 5-foot eye level, wide angle, late-afternoon golden hour]
* Text Brief Format: [e.g., room zones + markdown shopping table]

From a skill to an app

Today the system runs as a Claude skill plus the template above. The app would make it usable by anyone with a room to design.

BUILT

What exists

  • Six-pillar brief as a reusable skill
  • Floor-plan intake workflow
  • Manual scored audit
  • Hover-to-shop overlay on this page
PLANNED

Inputs

  • Home type: apartment or single-family house
  • Square footage, natural light and orientation
  • Materials: exposed brick, beams, tile or boards
  • Furniture, fixtures, lighting
  • Floor plan and the owner's own inspiration images
  • Any style, blended by ratio
PLANNED

Review and output

  • AI architect, contractor, and designer reviews of the layout and commonly overlooked items
  • A simple, minimal PDF lookbook with aerial-to-eye-level views of each room
  • A shopping list per room

The app is a concept and is not built. The audit is still done by hand, so automating it is the open problem.
